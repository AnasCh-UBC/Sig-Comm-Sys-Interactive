1Complex sinusoid: Review

Complex numbers and Euler’s formula

  1. Complex number in rectangular form: \(z=a+jb\), where \(a,b\in\mathbb R\) and \(j=\sqrt{-1}\)
  2. Complex number in polar form: \(z=re^{j\theta}=r\cos\theta+jr\sin\theta\) where \(r=|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) and \(\theta=\angle z\)
Complex conjugate: The complex conjugate of \(z\) is \(z^*=a-jb=re^{-j\theta}\)

The product of two complex numbers multiplies their magnitudes and adds their phases. Thus: \(zz^*=|z|^2=r\).

\[e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta,\qquad e^{-j\theta}=\cos\theta-j\sin\theta\] \[\cos\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}}{2},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j}\]

Complex sinusoid

The signal \(e^{j2\pi f_0t}\) is a complex sinusoidal signal.

It rotates counterclockwise around the unit circle with angular frequency \(\omega_0=2\pi f_0\) rad/s.

Its real and imaginary parts are cosine and sine with frequency \(f_0\), period \(T_0=\frac{1}{f_0}\):

\[e^{j2\pi f_0t}=\cos(2\pi f_0t)+j\sin(2\pi f_0t).\]

Synthesis: Periodic signals from harmonically related sinusoids

Signals of the form \(e^{j2\pi kf_0t}\), for integer \(k\), are harmonically related; the \(k\)-th harmonic has frequency \(kf_0\).

We can construct a periodic signal with frequency \(f_0\) using \(e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\) and its harmoics!

The sum \(x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x_k e^{j2\pi kf_0t}\) is called a Fourier series.

The coefficients \(x_k\) describe 'how much' of each harmonic is present. The constant term \(x_0\) is the average (DC) value.

3Fourier coefficients and spectra

Analysis: Calculating the coefficients

Given a periodic signal \(x(t)\), its Fourier coefficient at harmonic \(k\) is obtained by projecting \(x(t)\) onto \(e^{j2\pi kf_0t}\) over any one period.
\[x_k=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+T_0}x(t)e^{-j2\pi kf_0t}\,dt.\]

Because the integrand is periodic, the integration interval can start at any \(\tau\).

Tip: Select \(\tau\) to simplify the integration. A common choice is \(\tau=-\frac{T_0}{2}\) leading to the integration interval\([-\frac{T_0}{2},\frac{T_0}{2}]\), or \(\tau=0\) leading to \([0,T_0]\).

Example: A cosine

The Fourier coefficients of \(\cos(2\pi f_0 t)\) are \(x_1=x_{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\), and \(x_k=0,\forall k\neq \pm 1\).

Derivation of the Fourier coefficients

The complex Fourier-series coefficients are obtained from

\[ x_k=\frac{1}{T_0} \int_{0}^{T_0}x(t)e^{-jk2\pi k f_0t}\,dt. \]

Substitute \(x(t)=\cos(\omega_0t)\) and use Euler's identity:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_k &=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{0}^{T_0} \frac{1}{2} \left(e^{j2\pi f_0 t}+e^{-j2\pi f_0t}\right) e^{-j2\pi kf_0t}\,dt\\[6pt] &=\frac{1}{2T_0}\int_{0}^{T_0} \left[ e^{-j(k-1)2\pi f_0t} +e^{-j(k+1)2\pi f_0t} \right]dt\\[6pt] &=\frac{1}{2T_0}\int_{0}^{T_0} e^{-j(k-1)2\pi f_0t} dt+\frac{1}{2T_0}\int_{0}^{T_0} e^{-j(k+1)2\pi f_0t} dt. \end{aligned} \]

Note that:

\[ \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{0}^{T_0} e^{-j2\pi mf_0t}\,dt = \begin{cases} 1, & m=0,\\ 0, & m\ne 0, \end{cases} \]

Thus, the first integral is nonzero only when \(k=1\), and the second is nonzero only when \(k=-1\). Hence,

\[ x_k= \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{2}, & k=\pm 1,\\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Discrete spectrum

The Fourier coefficients \(x_k\) form a frequency-domain representation of \(x(t)\).

4Properties and conditions

Real-valued signals: Hermitian symmetry

If \(x(t)\) is real-valued, its Fourier coefficients satisfy \(x_{-k}=x_k^*.\)

Consequently, \(|x_k|\) is even and \(\angle x_k\) is odd.

A real signal can also be represented using cosine terms and phase shifts:

\[x(t)=x_0+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2|x_k|\cos(2\pi kf_0t+\angle x_k).\]

Trigonometric Fourier series

Real-valued signals have a trigonometic Fourier series representation.

\[x(t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[a_k\cos(2\pi kf_0t)+b_k\sin(2\pi kf_0t)\right],\]

where, over any one period \(a_k=\frac{2}{T_0}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+T_0}x(t)\cos(2\pi kf_0t)\,dt\) and \(b_k=\frac{2}{T_0}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+T_0}x(t)\sin(2\pi kf_0t)\,dt.\)

Even signals

For an even signal, the sine coefficients are zero. Its trigonometric Fourier series contains cosine terms only.

Odd signals

For an odd signal, the cosine coefficients (including DC) are zero. Its trigonometric Fourier series contains sine terms only.

Dirichlet conditions. These are sufficient (but not necessary) conditions for a Fourier series to exist: over one period, (1) \(x(t)\) is absolutely integrable, (2) it has a finite number of maxima and minima, and (3) it has a finite number of discontinuities.

Discontinuities and Gibbs phenomenon

Fourier series can represent periodic signals with jumps. Near a discontinuity, finite partial sums exhibit oscillatory overshoot; increasing the number of harmonics makes the oscillations narrower, but the peak overshoot does not disappear entirely.

5Fourier series and LTI systems

Periodic input to an LTI system

Complex exponentials are eigenfunctions of LTI systems. If the input is periodic with Fourier series \(x(t)=\sum_k x_ke^{j2\pi kf_0t}\), then the output is

\[\boxed{y(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x_kH(kf_0)e^{j2\pi kf_0t}.}\]

Here \(H(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt\) is the frequency response. The output coefficient at harmonic \(k\) is \(x_kH(kf_0)\). An LTI system changes the amplitude and phase of existing harmonics; it does not create new frequencies.

6Parseval’s relation

Power in time and frequency domains

The average power of a periodic signal equals the sum of the power in its Fourier-series components:

\[\boxed{P_x=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+T_0}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|x_k|^2.}\]

Power is conserved by the Fourier-series representation. We can calculate it either by averaging the squared time-domain waveform or by summing the squared magnitudes of its Fourier coefficients.
