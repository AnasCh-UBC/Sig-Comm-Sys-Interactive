1Fourier series to Fourier transform

From periodic to nonperiodic signals

Fourier series represents a periodic signal as a sum of harmonically related complex exponentials. For a nonperiodic signal, the frequency components are no longer restricted to discrete harmonics; they form a continuous spectrum.

\[X(f)=\mathcal{F}\{x(t)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt\]

The inverse Fourier transform reconstructs the time-domain signal:

\[x(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{X(f)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)e^{j2\pi ft}\,df.\]

The forward transform is frequency-domain analysis; the inverse transform is time-domain synthesis.

Angular-frequency form

Using angular frequency \(\omega=2\pi f\), the transform pair can also be written as

\[X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt,\qquad x(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(\omega)e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega.\]

In this course, we primarily use frequency \(f\) in hertz.

What do we plot?

Amplitude spectrum

\(|X(f)|\) versus frequency \(f\). It shows the strength of each frequency component.

Phase spectrum

\(\angle X(f)\) versus frequency \(f\). It shows the phase associated with each component.

The spectrum is generally complex-valued. An amplitude spectrum may also be displayed on a decibel scale.

Examples: rectangular and odd signals

For a rectangular pulse of duration \(\tau\),

\[x(t)=\Pi\!\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad X(f)=\tau\,\operatorname{sinc}(f\tau).\]

The transform is real and even, as expected for a real-even signal. For the real-odd signal \(x(t)=\sin(t)\Pi(t/\tau)\), the transform is imaginary and odd:

\[X(f)=\frac{\sin\!\left((2\pi f-1)\tau/2\right)}{j(2\pi f-1)}-\frac{\sin\!\left((2\pi f+1)\tau/2\right)}{j(2\pi f+1)}.\]

Here, \(\operatorname{sinc}(f)=\frac{\sin(\pi f)}{\pi f}\), with value 1 at \(f=0\). Also, \(\delta(t)\leftrightarrow1\) and \(1\leftrightarrow\delta(f)\).

Important observation: A short-duration or impulsive signal generally has a broad frequency spectrum. The extreme case is \(\delta(t)\), whose transform is constant at all frequencies.

2Properties of the Fourier transform

Fourier transform of real-valued signals

If \(x(t)\) is real-valued, then its transform has Hermitian symmetry:

\[X(-f)=X^*(f).\]
Even magnitude

\(|X(-f)|=|X(f)|\).

Odd phase

\(\angle X(-f)=-\angle X(f)\), where the phase is defined.

If the signal is also even, its transform is real and even. If the signal is real and odd, its transform is imaginary and odd.

Bandwidth

The bandwidth is the width of the smallest frequency interval \([f_L,f_H]\) outside which the amplitude spectrum is zero:

\[B=f_H-f_L.\]

For a baseband signal, \(f_L=0\), so its bandwidth is \(B=f_H\). In practical systems, spectra may not be strictly zero outside a finite interval; bandwidth is then defined using an agreed threshold.

Convolution and modulation

\[x(t)*y(t)\longleftrightarrow X(f)Y(f)\]

Convolution in time corresponds to multiplication in frequency. This is especially useful for analyzing LTI systems.

\[e^{j2\pi f_0t}x(t)\longleftrightarrow X(f-f_0)\]

Multiplying a signal by a complex sinusoid shifts its spectrum. This is called modulation or frequency shifting.

Parseval’s relation and autocorrelation

\[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)y^*(t)\,dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)Y^*(f)\,df.\]

For \(y(t)=x(t)\), the total signal energy is equal in the time and frequency domains:

\[E_x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|X(f)|^2df.\]

The autocorrelation is \(R_x(\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)x^*(t-\tau)dt\), and its Fourier transform is \(|X(f)|^2\), the energy spectral density.

Differentiation and integration

\[\frac{d^n x(t)}{dt^n}\longleftrightarrow(j2\pi f)^nX(f),\qquad t^n x(t)\longleftrightarrow\left(\frac{j}{2\pi}\right)^n\frac{d^nX(f)}{df^n}.\]

Time differentiation corresponds to multiplication by \(j2\pi f\). Multiplication by time corresponds to differentiation in frequency.

\[\int_{-\infty}^t x(\tau)d\tau\longleftrightarrow\frac{X(f)}{j2\pi f}+\frac{1}{2}X(0)\delta(f).\]

4Fourier transform and LTI systems

Transmission through an LTI system

For an LTI system with impulse response \(h(t)\), input \(x(t)\), and output \(y(t)=h(t)*x(t)\), the convolution property gives

\[Y(f)=H(f)X(f).\]

Frequency-domain analysis often simplifies system analysis: convolution becomes multiplication, and each frequency component is scaled and phase-shifted by \(H(f)\).

A filter may also have a linear phase term \(e^{-j2\pi ft_d}\) in its passband and still be considered ideal: it delays the signal by \(t_d\).

A nonideal filter has a frequency response that varies within its passband. Its 3-dB cutoff frequency is where the power gain is half its maximum, equivalently where the magnitude is \(1/\sqrt2\) of its maximum.
